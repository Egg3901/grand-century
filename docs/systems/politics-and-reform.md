# Politics and reform

Politics is what stands between you and the changes you want to make. You do not
simply enact a reform; you have to carry an upper house that has its own
composition, its own ideology, and a growing impatience with being asked.

The whole system runs once a month, after culture and before research.

## Parties and the upper house

Each nation has a set of parties, each with an ideology. The **upper house** is a
distribution of seats across those ideologies, and it is what actually votes on
reform.

Pops have ideologies too, derived from their type and circumstances. In an
elective government, elections pick a ruling party out of that pop base. In a
non-elective one they do not — but the upper house still exists, still has a
composition, and still drifts over time.

**Franchise** determines whose opinion counts. Each pop type supports the
franchise differently at each level, so widening the franchise changes which pops
translate into political weight. This is the lever that changes who the upper
house represents, and it is itself a reform.

## Passing a reform

To pass a reform you need enough **support in the upper house** to clear the
**threshold** for that reform's category under your form of government. The
threshold varies: an autocracy and a parliamentary state do not need the same
level of agreement to change the same law.

Reforms also cost money and prestige, scaling with the level you are reaching for.

## Reform fatigue

This is the mechanic that stops reform being a checklist.

Every reform you pass adds **fatigue**, and fatigue does two things: it reduces
the support any future reform can muster (by up to 20%), and it raises the cost
of reform (by up to an additional 125%). The gain per reform has a base component,
a per-level component, and a category-specific component — so some kinds of change
tire a nation faster than others.

Fatigue decays at 12% per month. It is a rate limit, not a wall: a nation that
reforms hard for a year will find the next reform expensive and poorly supported,
and will need to let the country settle before pushing again.

## Unrest, suppression and rebellion

Pops generate **reform demand**: each pop, based on its consciousness and
militancy, wants a particular reform. Aggregate that across a nation and you have
a picture of what the country is actually agitating for.

You can respond in two directions. Granting the demanded reform relieves the
pressure. Or you can apply **political suppression**, which pushes back on
militancy and consciousness directly — but suppression is a policy with its own
costs, and it treats the symptom.

Unmet demand accumulates as **state unrest**. Where unrest runs high enough, a
**rebellion** spawns with a specific demand attached to it. Rebels are not random
banditry; they want something, and it is something your pops were already asking
for.

Note the ordering within the monthly pass: suppression is applied first, and
unrest is then recalculated from the post-suppression state of your pops. What
you see as unrest already accounts for the boot on the neck.

## Literacy and consciousness

Literacy and national consciousness are recomputed monthly. Literacy feeds
[research](research.md) directly — an illiterate nation researches slowly.
Consciousness feeds reform demand. This is the central tension of the century:
the educated population that powers your science is the same population that
starts demanding a say.

## What this means in play

- **You cannot reform continuously.** Fatigue makes the second and third reform
  of a year progressively harder and dearer. Space them.
- **Widen the franchise deliberately.** It changes who the upper house answers to,
  which changes what you can pass afterwards. It is a move with consequences in
  both directions.
- **Rebellion is a forecast, not a surprise.** Aggregate reform demand tells you
  what your pops want before unrest turns into rebels wanting the same thing.
- **Suppression buys time, not consent.** It lowers militancy without addressing
  the demand that produced it.
- **Educating your country is a political act.** Literacy is the engine of
  research and of consciousness at the same time.
